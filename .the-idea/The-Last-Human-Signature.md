# The Last Human Signature

## When machines can make almost anything, the defining human act will be taking responsibility

**By Eric Mourant +AI**

*Proposer of `+AI` as a situational responsibility post-nominal*

---

At the end of every chain of human authority, there has traditionally been a name.

A ruler placed a seal beneath a decree. A merchant signed a ledger. An architect marked a drawing. An author placed a byline above an argument. A developer attached an identity to a software contribution.

The instruments changed. The underlying act did not.

A signature brought consequence home.

It transformed an anonymous object—a command, contract, design, payment, promise or proposition—into something for which a particular person could be praised, questioned, trusted, challenged or held to account.

The signature did not necessarily mean that the signer had personally performed every part of the work. Executives signed reports assembled by teams. Architects approved drawings developed by specialists. Editors published words shaped by many hands. Generals authorised plans they did not personally calculate.

The signature meant something more durable than sole creation:

> **This has passed through my judgement. I have chosen to put my name behind it.**

Artificial intelligence now unsettles the assumptions beneath that act.

A machine can research a subject, propose an argument, analyse data, write a report, generate an image, compose music, produce software, test alternatives and revise its own output. Increasingly, an AI agent can move beyond creation and begin to act: sending messages, making bookings, negotiating terms, executing transactions or deploying code.

The chain of production can become almost entirely non-human.

Yet somewhere, before the result enters the world, there remains a final question:

# **Who takes responsibility?**

The answer to that question may become the last recognisably human signature.

---

## The wrong question

Much of the present debate about artificial intelligence begins with a question that appears simple:

> **Was this made by a human or by AI?**

It is already becoming the wrong question.

Consider a report developed through a long exchange between Eric Mourant and an AI system.

Eric identifies the problem.

The AI proposes a structure.

Eric rejects the first approach and supplies context.

The AI analyses the available information.

Eric notices an invalid assumption.

The AI recalculates.

Eric reframes the objective.

The AI drafts the report.

Eric changes the conclusion.

The AI compresses the language.

Eric decides that the result is ready to be released.

Who wrote it?

One answer might be that both did. Another might be that the AI generated most of the words while Eric supplied most of the judgement. A third might distinguish between composition, authorship, editing, direction and approval.

None resolves the problem.

A percentage would be even less useful. Word count cannot measure intellectual contribution. A machine might generate ninety per cent of the text while the human contributes the single decision that gives the work its meaning. Or the human might write almost everything while AI supplies one analysis on which the entire conclusion depends.

Hybrid creation is not a clean division of labour.

It is a loop.

Human intent becomes machine output. Machine output becomes human judgement. Human judgement becomes a new instruction. The process continues until the distinction between originator, collaborator, tool, editor and author becomes difficult to describe.

The more useful question is not:

> Who generated every component?

It is:

# **Who adopted the whole?**

Who chose to send it?

Who authorised its publication?

Who approved the recommendation?

Who deployed the code?

Who attached a name to the outcome?

The old question attempts to reconstruct the past.

The new question establishes responsibility in the present.

---

## Three characters after a name

The `+AI` proposal begins with a deliberately small act:

# **Eric Mourant +AI**

The notation is placed directly after the name of the person or organisation accepting responsibility for the associated work.

It means:

# **AI helped. I take responsibility.**

The plus sign matters.

It does not suggest that Eric Mourant is an artificial intelligence, holds an AI qualification or has surrendered his identity to a machine.

It expresses a relationship:

**Human + artificial intelligence.**

It represents augmentation rather than replacement.

The human brings intention, context, values, authority and judgement. The AI contributes analysis, generation, transformation, scale or speed. The resulting work may contain both in proportions that cannot sensibly be measured.

But the name remains first.

Responsibility remains attached to the named human.

When Eric writes:

**Eric Mourant +AI**

he is not claiming that he personally originated every sentence, calculation, pixel or line of code.

He is declaring that AI materially assisted the work, that he chose to adopt the result in the form presented, and that he accepts responsibility for releasing it.

This is not merely a disclosure of technology.

It is a declaration of ownership.

---

## The novelty must be stated honestly

An idea concerned with transparency must be transparent about its own origins.

Eric Mourant did not invent the character sequence `Name + AI`, nor should the proposal be presented as the first occasion on which someone placed those symbols after an author’s name.

On 12 December 2022, Timothy Baril suggested the label **“by Author Name + AI”** in a Royal Road discussion about disclosing AI involvement in creative work. That is clear prior art for the typography and for its use as an AI disclosure label. The supplied novelty analysis therefore correctly rejects any broad claim that Mourant invented the notation itself or was the first person to place `+ AI` after a name.

Nor is the principle of human responsibility for AI-assisted work new.

Scientific publishing rules already state that AI tools cannot qualify as authors because they cannot assume responsibility for a work’s accuracy, integrity and originality; responsibility remains with the human authors who use them.

Microsoft’s MSN policy recommends bylines such as **“Jane Doe (AI-assisted)”** or **“By Jane Doe (with AI)”** specifically to identify the human accountable for AI-assisted material.

The Linux kernel’s guidance makes the distinction operational: an AI contribution receives an `Assisted-by` tag, while only a human may add the legally consequential `Signed-off-by` tag and take full responsibility for the contribution.

Most significantly, the 2025 Human-Generated Content Protocol defined `human+ai` expression and an `HGCP-H+AI` responsibility level. Its AI-assisted example combines a signer’s identity, AI assistance, human review and an explicit declaration of human responsibility.

These precedents contain nearly all the ingredients.

What, then, is distinctive about Mourant’s proposal?

Not the plus sign.

Not AI disclosure.

Not human responsibility.

Not even the broad combination of human identity and AI assistance.

The proposed contribution is the **compression of those elements into a new semantic object**:

# **`Name +AI` as the declaration itself.**

Earlier approaches tend to use a disclosure phrase, a parenthetical byline, separate attribution fields, a badge, a metadata structure or a longer signature block.

The `+AI` post-nominal reduces the relationship to:

# **identity + responsibility mark**

Its novelty is therefore semantic and structural.

It transforms:

**AI disclosure → accountable human declaration**

and:

**disclosure label → situational responsibility post-nominal**

The novelty analysis identifies precisely this narrower territory: no earlier proposal was found that defined `+AI`, appended directly to a human or organisational name, as a situational post-nominal that itself declares both material AI assistance and the named party’s acceptance of responsibility for the associated work.

That is the defensible claim.

It does not attempt to own the punctuation.

It defines what the punctuation does.

---

## A post-nominal of action

Traditional post-nominals usually describe something relatively enduring about a person.

They may indicate an academic degree, professional standing, public honour, membership or institutional recognition.

They look backwards.

They tell us what someone has earned, completed or been authorised to practise.

`+AI` is different.

It does not say what Eric Mourant **is**.

It says what Eric Mourant is declaring **about this work**.

That makes it a situational post-nominal.

A doctorate remains after a person’s name regardless of whether a particular email concerns their field of study. A professional designation ordinarily persists across many contexts.

`+AI` should appear only when the associated artifact was materially assisted by artificial intelligence and the named party accepts responsibility for it.

Eric might sign one communication:

**Eric Mourant +AI**

and another:

**Eric Mourant**

The first is not a higher rank.

The second is not a denial of technology.

Each notation relates to a particular act of communication.

This quality gives `+AI` unusual precision. It is post-nominal in position, but declarative in function.

It is less like a qualification than a compact verb.

It means:

> **I used. I adopted. I release. I answer.**

---

## Authorship is separating from responsibility

Artificial intelligence exposes a distinction that has always existed but was often hidden:

**Creating something is not identical to taking responsibility for it.**

A person can be responsible for a decision without personally calculating every variable behind it.

A publisher can be responsible for material written by others.

A director can take responsibility for a film created by hundreds of contributors.

An engineering authority can approve a design assembled through the work of many specialists and software systems.

Responsibility does not always follow from generating every component.

It can also arise through direction, selection, adoption and release.

AI makes that distinction impossible to ignore.

Suppose an AI system generates nearly all the words in a proposal. Eric Mourant reviews it, corrects it, accepts its argument and sends it under his own authority.

It may be inaccurate to say that Eric wrote every word.

It is not inaccurate to say that Eric chose to issue the proposal.

The relevant act is adoption.

`+AI` does not solve every philosophical or legal question about authorship. It does something more practical: it identifies the person who has decided that the artifact may proceed under their name.

That person may not be the sole originator.

They are the responsible signatory.

This is a modern form of authorship—not authorship as solitary generation, but authorship as informed adoption.

---

## Not a confession

The language surrounding AI disclosure often carries the tone of an admission:

> AI was used here.

The implication is that something questionable has occurred and must be confessed.

That framing will not survive.

Artificial intelligence is becoming embedded in operating systems, writing tools, search systems, design software, development environments, cameras, communications platforms and analytical applications. Its presence will often be ordinary, ambient and difficult to separate from the wider process of work.

The meaningful distinction will not be between those who have remained untouched by AI and those who have not.

It will be between those who use powerful systems while remaining accountable and those who use them to create distance from consequences.

`+AI` is not an apology.

It does not say:

> Forgive me; a machine was involved.

It says:

> I am telling you that a machine materially helped because I am not hiding the process. I am putting my name here because I am not hiding from the result.

The mark therefore rejects two opposite evasions.

The first is the pretence of unaided authorship.

The second is the pretence that AI involvement dissolves human responsibility.

Both are forms of disappearance.

`+AI` keeps the human visible.

---

## You cannot keep the brilliance and outsource the mistake

The central ethical force of the notation lies in a simple asymmetry.

People are often willing to accept credit for excellent AI-assisted work while blaming the machine when the same process produces an error.

If the report is insightful:

> I produced it.

If the report is wrong:

> The AI hallucinated.

If the software works:

> I built it.

If the software fails:

> The model generated that code.

If the image is praised:

> My creation.

If it causes harm:

> The system made it.

This arrangement cannot hold.

You cannot claim the machine’s brilliance and disclaim its mistakes.

You chose the tool.

You shaped the instruction.

You selected the output.

You decided that it was ready.

You released it into a human environment where its consequences would be borne by other people.

# **You used the machine. You own the result.**

That sentence does not mean that AI providers, system designers or institutions can never bear responsibility. Many actors may contribute to an outcome, and different forms of legal, technical and organisational accountability may coexist.

The point is narrower.

The person who puts their name on an AI-assisted artifact cannot use the machine’s involvement to erase their own agency.

`+AI` makes that principle visible at the moment of publication.

---

## Responsibility is not infallibility

A useful responsibility mark must not promise more than a human being can honestly deliver.

`+AI` does not mean that the associated work is correct.

It does not mean that every statement has been independently verified.

It does not mean that every citation is valid, every calculation is error-free or every risk has been eliminated.

It is not a security certification.

It is not a guarantee of originality.

It does not prove that no confidential information entered an AI system.

It does not declare that the signer can reproduce the machine’s internal process.

It does not transform judgement into certainty.

Human signatures have never guaranteed perfection.

A signed argument can be mistaken. A signed design can fail. A signed contract can contain an ambiguity. A signed report can omit something important.

The purpose of the signature is not to make error impossible.

It is to make responsibility locatable.

So too with `+AI`.

The declaration is not:

> This work cannot be wrong.

It is:

> If this work is wrong, I do not disappear behind the mechanism that helped produce it.

That distinction is essential.

A mark that implied perfect verification would be too dangerous to use honestly. A mark that signified nothing more than the presence of software would be too weak to matter.

`+AI` occupies the space between those extremes:

**material assistance, conscious adoption, identifiable responsibility.**

---

## Material assistance, not microscopic disclosure

The notation should not be triggered merely because a device corrected a spelling error or supplied a routine formatting function.

The useful threshold is materiality.

Did AI materially affect the substance, reasoning, expression, design, analysis, recommendation, code or outcome?

Did it contribute something that a reasonable reader, recipient or user might consider relevant to understanding how the work was produced?

The test should not be based on a percentage.

A small amount of AI contribution may be highly material. A large amount of automated activity may be merely mechanical.

Nor should the standard demand a forensic accounting of every interaction.

The visible mark answers the central human question.

More detailed information—model identity, tool version, prompts, modification history, review status or cryptographic provenance—can exist in metadata where the context requires it.

The post-nominal remains simple because ordinary social conventions must be easy to use.

**Eric Mourant +AI**

Three characters after a name.

A full declaration behind them.

---

## From a visible mark to provenance infrastructure

The simplicity of `+AI` does not prevent a more sophisticated technical layer from developing beneath it.

Digital provenance standards such as C2PA already provide cryptographically bound structures capable of recording the origin and modification history of digital assets, including assertions about AI use. Importantly, C2PA records provenance without declaring whether the content itself is good, true or trustworthy.

That leaves room for a complementary human declaration.

A future document might visibly display:

**Eric Mourant +AI**

while its metadata records:

* the identity of the responsible party;
* when the declaration was made;
* which AI systems materially contributed;
* whether the complete artifact was reviewed;
* which transformations occurred;
* whether the declaration was digitally signed;
* and whether the artifact has changed since acceptance.

The visible mark would serve the reader.

The structured record would serve software, institutions and verification systems.

This is the potential progression:

# **symbol → convention → post-nominal → protocol → infrastructure**

The human-facing symbol should not attempt to contain the entire provenance history.

Its purpose is to preserve the part that must never be lost inside technical complexity:

# **the responsible name**

---

## The problem becomes greater when AI begins to act

Content generation is only the first stage.

The larger transformation begins when artificial intelligence moves from producing artifacts to taking consequential actions.

An AI agent may draft a message and send it.

It may compare suppliers and place an order.

It may monitor a system and deploy a change.

It may negotiate an appointment, respond to a complaint, move funds within authorised limits or commit an organisation to a course of action.

At that point, disclosure that “AI was involved” is radically insufficient.

The important questions become:

Who authorised the agent?

What authority was delegated?

What limits were imposed?

Who monitored its behaviour?

Who accepted the resulting action?

Who answers when the system acts outside expectations?

The danger of the agentic age is not merely that machines will become capable.

It is that human responsibility may become distributed so widely that no one appears to possess it.

An engineer blames the model.

A manager blames the implementation.

A company blames the agent.

A user blames the platform.

The platform blames the instructions.

Responsibility evaporates into the architecture.

A mature `+AI` system could become one mechanism for preventing that disappearance.

It would not mean that one individual is responsible for every technical cause. It would identify the human or organisation willing to say:

> This system acted under my authority. I accept responsibility for the decision to authorise and release the result.

That is not merely provenance.

It is governance.

---

## The mark beside an organisation

The responsible party need not always be an individual.

An organisation might issue an artifact as:

# **JAPER Technology +AI**

The declaration would then attach to the organisation’s authority and governance rather than to one person acting privately.

Where stronger specificity is needed, both levels could appear:

**JAPER Technology**
**Eric Mourant +AI**

This preserves a distinction between institutional responsibility and personal approval.

The same principle can apply to a research group, publisher, public authority, software project or professional practice.

What matters is that the named party is real enough to answer.

The mark should never become a decorative badge attached to autonomous output merely to create the appearance of human oversight.

Without meaningful adoption, `+AI` would be false.

The name must remain consequential.

---

## A social convention before a formal standard

The most important technologies are not always complicated.

Sometimes they are shared habits.

A convention succeeds when people can reproduce it without specialist tools, institutional permission or technical training.

`+AI` works in email, documents, source code, image captions, reports, presentations, profiles, printed publications and plain text.

It requires no registration.

It depends on no particular AI provider.

It can survive conversion between file formats.

It can be handwritten.

It can be read aloud.

It can be copied in seconds.

At first, the recipient of an email may pause at:

**Kindest regards,**
**Eric Mourant +AI**

They may ask what it means.

The answer takes one sentence:

# **AI helped. I take responsibility.**

The next time they see it, the mark is familiar.

After repeated use, it becomes a convention.

Only then does the deeper infrastructure become possible. Social meaning must precede technical enforcement. A machine-readable field is valuable only when human beings agree on what the field means.

The first task is therefore not to build the most elaborate protocol.

It is to establish the sentence.

## The last human act

Imagine a future production system.

AI identifies the opportunity.

AI performs the research.

AI models the alternatives.

AI writes the proposal.

AI produces the illustrations.

AI generates and tests the code.

AI prepares the launch materials.

AI schedules the release.

Perhaps the human did not personally create any individual component.

What remains?

The human may still determine the objective.

The human may still set the boundaries.

The human may still decide whether the system should proceed.

And at the end, the human may still choose whether to attach a name.

That final act is not trivial.

It is the point where capability becomes consequence.

It is where possibility becomes decision.

It is where an output becomes an undertaking.

The last human signature does not say:

> I made every part of this.

It says:

> I will not use the complexity of its creation as a place to hide.

That may become the essential meaning of authorship in the AI age.

Not solitary production.

Not technological purity.

Not a fictional claim that the machine was absent.

But transparent augmentation joined to human adoption.

The future may be written by many forms of intelligence.

Responsibility will still need a name.

# **Eric Mourant +AI**

# **AI helped. I take responsibility.**

# **You used the machine. You own the result.**

# **+AI**

## Note on the novelty claim

No claim is made that Eric Mourant invented the symbols `+AI`, first placed them after a name, first proposed disclosure of AI assistance, or first connected AI-assisted work with human accountability.

The narrower proposal is that `+AI`, when appended directly to a human or organisational name, functions as a **situational responsibility post-nominal**: a compact declaration that AI materially assisted the associated work and that the named party has adopted and accepts responsibility for its release.

As of the documented novelty analysis, no earlier proposal establishing that specific construction and meaning had been identified.
